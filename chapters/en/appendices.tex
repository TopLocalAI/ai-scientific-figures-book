\chapter{Figure Brief Templates}
\label{app:brief-templates}

Writing down your research intent as a structured figure brief before opening a drawing canvas or typing a prompt into \scidraw{} is the central working habit of this book. A figure brief is not bureaucratic paperwork for software; it is an explicit contract among you, your co-authors, peer reviewers, and AI tools. It defines the question the figure must answer, the empirical evidence that is off-limits to alteration, the visual degrees of freedom open to exploration, and the objective criteria by which the finished figure will be judged. This appendix brings together the general ten-field template introduced in Chapter 2, four specialized variants tailored to distinct figure genres, and a fully populated brief for an A0 conference poster.

\section{General Ten-Field Template}

The ten fields below are ordered so that research goals and evidentiary boundaries are locked in before visual styling and delivery specifications are chosen. Whenever you start a new scientific figure, copy the block below and fill in every field; mark any item that has not yet been finalized as ``To be confirmed'' rather than deleting the line.

\begin{Prompt}
[Use and audience] Where the figure will appear (journal main text / supplementary information / lab-meeting slide / conference poster / graphical abstract), who the target readers are, and what domain background they already share.
[One-sentence goal] After viewing this figure, the reader should understand ____, and be able to point directly to ____ in the figure.
[Materials and sources] Identifiers, version numbers, sheet names, or page numbers of the underlying data tables, raw images, or literature sources; open license or permission status; date collected or accessed.
[Required content] Every study object, panel, data series, experimental group, or structural layer that must appear in the figure, with explicit counts for each.
[Relationships and reading order] Logical, physical, or temporal relationships among objects (source -> target -> meaning -> visual encoding -> experimental or literature basis), together with the intended eye-movement path across the figure.
[Evidence status] Item-by-item classification into measured observation, mechanistic interpretation, working hypothesis, or unverified inference, plus the exact wording used to label simulated data or model-generated illustrations inside the figure and in the legend.
[Visual plan] Chart or diagram genre, spatial grid layout, semantic colour roles, label language, and target physical dimensions (final printed width in mm, minimum font size in pt, line width in pt).
[Immutable constraints] Numerical values, physical units, pairing structures, axis limits, colour-role assignments, and verified scientific relationships that neither software tools nor layout edits are permitted to alter.
[Delivery requirements and date checked] Official figure guidelines of the target journal, conference, or institution (guideline title and date checked), required delivery formats, and file-naming convention.
[Acceptance criteria] Quantifiable, auditable pass/fail checks, stating who performs each check and at what physical scale or viewing mode.
\end{Prompt}

\begin{BookNote}
Completion tip: Field 2 (``One-sentence goal'') must name a concrete piece of visual evidence that a reader can physically point to in the figure (for example, ``locate the inflection time of the control group''), rather than a vague intention such as ``show experimental results.'' Field 8 (``Immutable constraints'') serves directly as the ``Keep unchanged'' block in every subsequent multi-round revision instruction.
\end{BookNote}

\section{Four Specialized Variants}

Different genres of scientific figures rest on different forms of evidence. When working on the four figure types below, expand or adapt the general ten-field template with the following specialized entries:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Data-plot variant (corresponding to Chapter 5):} Add three fields to the general template:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item {[Unit of observation and pairing/grouping]} State what a single data point represents (for example, one daily watershed sample, one individual animal, or one independent fabrication batch) and whether measurements come from independent groups or paired before-and-after observations.
    \item {[Data processing]} Specify filtering criteria, coordinate transformations (such as logarithmic axes), normalization baselines, and rules for handling missing values; never drop outliers without explicit documentation.
    \item {[Statistical representation]} Define the exact summary statistic and error-bar convention (standard deviation SD, standard error of the mean SEM, or 95\% confidence interval CI); sample size $n$ and hypothesis-test outputs must be supplied by the author from verified analysis logs and must never be estimated or invented by AI.
  \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Mechanism and workflow variant (corresponding to Chapters 6 and 7):} Strengthen and add three fields:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item {[Relationships and reading order]} Expand this field into a structured ``Object and Relationship Table'' listing ``Source object --- Relationship verb --- Target object --- Evidence status --- Line and arrowhead style'' row by row; every decision node in a workflow diagram must include both ``Pass'' and ``Fail'' exit branches.
    \item {[Line-style semantics]} Assign a single, unambiguous meaning to solid lines, dashed lines, dotted lines, filled arrowheads, flat T-bar inhibition caps, and transmembrane translocation arrows, and include a matching key in the figure or legend.
    \item {[Omissions and simplifications]} Record collateral pathways, cofactors, or secondary steps deliberately omitted to control visual density, and carry those notes into the figure legend.
  \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Experimental-image panel variant (corresponding to Chapter 8):} Add three fields to the general template:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item {[Raw-image inventory and checksums]} List the read-only original image identifiers, acquisition channels, bit depth, pixel dimensions, and file hash checksums so that primary data are never overwritten during layout.
    \item {[Calibration and scale-bar basis]} Record the objective magnification, physical pixel size (for example, micrometres per pixel), and the arithmetic converting physical length into scale-bar pixel width, never relying on AI to guess a scale bar.
    \item {[Permitted and prohibited processing]} State permitted full-frame operations (such as bounded cropping, linear full-frame brightness and contrast mapping, and colour-vision-accessible pseudocolour channel assignment applied with identical intensity windows across all compared groups) alongside strictly prohibited operations (local cloning or erasure, generative inpainting or redrawing, and AI super-resolution upscaling).
  \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Oral-presentation and academic-poster variant (corresponding to Chapter 12):} Add five fields to the general template:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item {[Viewing distance and dwell time]} Record the distance from the back row to the projection screen together with screen height (for talks), or the corridor walk-by distance and close-up reading distance (for posters), along with the audience's expected dwell time per slide or poster.
    \item {[Pacing control]} Distinguish speaker-paced delivery (where progressive builds guide attention step by step) from reader-paced browsing (where the poster must stand on its own when the presenter steps away).
    \item {[One assertion headline per slide or block]} Decompose the multiple sub-questions of a manuscript composite figure so that each slide or poster conclusion block carries a single data-backed assertion sentence as its heading.
    \item {[Minimum font size and line width]} Use the visual-angle scaling formula to set hard lower bounds on body text, axis tick labels, and curve stroke widths for the venue geometry.
    \item {[Items locked to the manuscript figure]} Lock every numerical value, physical quantity, unit, axis range, semantic colour assignment, and evidence-status label (``Simulated data'', ``Model-generated illustration'') to match the manuscript version exactly.
  \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\section{Completed Example: A0 Portrait Poster Brief for Perovskite Solar Cells}

Below is the complete figure brief used in Chapter 12 to adapt the planar perovskite solar cell interfacial passivation case study into an A0 portrait conference poster, followed by three verification annotations.

\begin{Prompt}
[Use and audience] Materials and energy conference poster session, ISO A0 portrait (841 mm x 1189 mm); passers-by at 2 to 5 m decide within a few seconds whether to stop, while engaged visitors read at 0.5 to 1.5 m for 3 to 5 minutes; the author may not be present at every moment to explain the board.
[One-sentence goal] From 3 m away, visitors can read the research question and main conclusion directly (interfacial passivation simultaneously improves simulated initial efficiency and operational stability); stepping to 1 to 1.5 m, they can inspect three core evidence blocks in sequence---"where the device was modified," "how much efficiency increased," and "how much stability improved"---together with essential methodological context.
[Materials and sources] Built from the four companion assets of Manuscript Figure 2 in Chapter 9: M1 device-stack schematic base illustration, M2 scanning-electron-microscopy-style cross-sectional placeholder (model-generated illustration), M3 single-diode simulated current density-voltage data table and summary performance metrics table, and M4 simulated 1000 h normalized efficiency decay table (original teaching materials of this book, released under CC BY 4.0 / MIT).
[Required content] Top title banner (research question and headline conclusion); middle conclusion tier with three blocks (Block 1: seven-layer planar n-i-p stack and passivation-layer position; Block 2: control vs. modified simulated current density-voltage curves with open-circuit voltage, short-circuit current density, fill factor, and power conversion efficiency comparison; Block 3: 1000 h normalized efficiency trajectories and time to 80% of initial efficiency T80); bottom detail tier (key simulation equations and test conditions, small cross-sectional schematic, references, and open-material notice).
[Relationships and reading order] Two-column vertical layout grid with 30 mm outer margins and a 20 mm inter-column gutter; reading order proceeds from the full-width top banner -> upper-left block (device modification) -> lower-left block (initial efficiency gain) -> upper-right block (long-term operational stability) -> lower-right block (simulation parameters, cross-sectional schematic, and references), avoiding back-and-forth Z-pattern eye jumps.
[Evidence status] All performance curves and metrics come from deterministic single-diode and double-exponential decay equations and must carry an explicit in-plot "Simulated data" label; the cross-sectional morphology graphic is a generative placeholder and must carry an in-panel "Model-generated illustration, not a micrograph" label in the lower-left corner; the device stack carries "Schematic, not to scale."
[Visual plan] Main banner title >= 90 pt, subtitle >= 44 pt; each conclusion block pairs an assertion heading (>= 44 pt) with a dedicated chart replotted for a 1.5 m viewing distance (axis tick labels >= 28 pt, axis titles >= 32 pt, curve stroke width >= 3.0 pt, direct end-of-curve labels replacing detached legend boxes); detail-tier body text >= 28 pt and references >= 24 pt; semantic colours match the manuscript figure (control in dark grey #6E6E6E dashed line, modified in deep teal #1B7F86 solid line).
[Immutable constraints] Simulated metrics are locked to the verified summary table (Control: Jsc = 22.46 mA cm^-2, Voc = 1.100 V, FF = 0.777, PCE = 19.21%, T80 = 210 h, 1000 h retention 47%; Modified: Jsc = 22.78 mA cm^-2, Voc = 1.140 V, FF = 0.808, PCE = 20.98%, T80 > 1000 h, 1000 h retention 86%); axis quantities, units, and limits (voltage 0 to 1.2 V, current density 0 to 25 mA cm^-2, time 0 to 1000 h) and all evidence-status labels must remain intact.
[Delivery requirements and date checked] Export a full-size A0 portrait vector PDF with all fonts embedded, accompanied by a 12 cm tall thumbnail image for density auditing; record production date (2026-10-29).
[Acceptance criteria] (1) Thumbnail and squint test: when scaled to 12 cm height and lightly Gaussian-blurred, the top banner and three conclusion blocks remain visually distinct; (2) 1:1 physical tile printout test: printing any single chart block at 100% scale on an A4 sheet and taping it to a wall allows effortless reading of tick labels and direct annotations from 1.5 m; (3) Three-version consistency audit: the author verifies every number, unit, colour, and evidence-status label against the manuscript figure and slide deck.
\end{Prompt}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Annotation 1 (making the one-sentence goal testable):} The goal field separates far-field reading (grasping the headline conclusion from $3\text{ m}$) from near-field reading (locating three evidence blocks from $1\text{--}1.5\text{ m}$). During review, you simply ask a colleague standing at those distances, ``Which layer of the device was modified, and how much did efficiency increase?'' If they can answer without prompting, the layout passes.
  \item \textbf{Annotation 2 (preventing numerical drift via immutable constraints):} Field 8 writes out all six summary metrics for both groups along with the exact axis ranges. When replotting poster-scale charts in the scientific data visualization module of \scidraw{}, this block is pasted directly as a leading constraint so that any drift in curve knee points or text callouts is caught immediately.
  \item \textbf{Annotation 3 (replacing subjective screen checks with thumbnail and print tests):} Rather than zooming in on a laptop monitor and guessing that the text ``looks big enough,'' combining a $12\text{ cm}$ thumbnail density check with a $1{:}1$ A4 tile printout viewed from $1.5\text{ m}$ catches overcrowded panels and undersized type before you pay for large-format printing.
\end{itemize}

\chapter{Prompt and Revision-Instruction Rewrite Table}
\label{app:prompt-rewrites}

During figure preparation and co-author review, the most common feedback consists of subjective impressions: ``it doesn't look professional,'' ``it's too cluttered,'' ``the colours feel off,'' or ``fix the top-left corner.'' Such vague requests fail whether you hand them to an AI tool or a junior colleague, because neither knows which elements are locked constraints and what quantitative change constitutes success. This appendix summarizes the fixed three-part revision structure established in Chapter 11, compiles a master rewrite table covering 26 typical vague critiques across Chapters 1--13, and lists five core rules for writing English base-illustration prompts.

\section{Fixed Structure for Revision Instructions and Issue Prioritization}

Whenever you evaluate a draft figure, classify every flaw using the three-tier priority hierarchy from Chapter 11 before issuing instructions: \textbf{Priority 1 covers scientific errors and academic integrity risks} (distorted values, reversed causal arrows, missing ``Simulated data'' or ``Model-generated illustration'' labels, or fabricated scale bars); \textbf{Priority 2 covers reading barriers} (broken eye-movement order, text that falls below legibility thresholds at final print width, or groups that merge in grayscale or under colour-vision deficiency); \textbf{Priority 3 covers visual consistency and aesthetic polish} (margin alignment, uniform stroke weights, and removal of decorative clutter). Once issues are prioritized, format every revision instruction sent to a tool or logged in your version history using three mandatory blocks:

\begin{Prompt}
Keep unchanged: Explicitly list the object names, counts, numerical values, axis ranges, topological connections, semantic colour assignments, and in-figure evidence-status labels that must not be altered in this round.
Modify only: Focus on one to three concrete edits per round, naming the exact spatial location (for example, "lower-left panel" or "right end of the curve"), target object, and quantitative parameters (font size in pt, stroke width in pt, hex colour code).
Verify after completion: List the pass/fail checks the author will perform on the new output, including a side-by-side comparison against the previous version to confirm that no unintended drift occurred elsewhere.
\end{Prompt}

\section{Key Principles for Writing English Base-Illustration Prompts}

When using prompt drawing, sketch mode, or mechanism figure generation in \scidraw{} (which call underlying image-generation models) to create structural or mechanistic base illustrations, English prompts frequently provide tighter compositional control. Follow five rules whenever you draft an English prompt for a visual base layer:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Explicitly prohibit all text and symbols (No-text rule):} Image generation models lack a deterministic typesetting engine; allowing them to render labels almost always produces misspelled pseudo-words or garbled characters. End every base-image prompt with: \texttt{Strictly no text of any kind: no letters, no words, no numbers, no labels, no scale bar, no watermark.} Add all scientific labels afterward in a vector layer.
  \item \textbf{State aspect ratio and background upfront (Aspect ratio and background):} Open the prompt by declaring the exact canvas proportion and clean background, for example: \texttt{Clean flat vector-style scientific schematic, landscape 16:9, pure white background, generous margin on the right for labels.}
  \item \textbf{Describe structure using relative spatial coordinates (Relative spatial layout):} Walk through the scene in an explicit geometric order---such as \texttt{From bottom to top}, \texttt{From left to right}, or \texttt{From extracellular space across the plasma membrane into the cytoplasm}---specifying the shape, relative thickness, and colour of each component while reserving open whitespace for leader lines and text callouts.
  \item \textbf{Request a single view per prompt (Single view per prompt):} Never ask an image model to render ``a regional watershed map, a magnified cross-section, and a right-hand line plot'' inside a single prompt. Generate or plot each view independently according to its evidence rules, then assemble them in a multi-panel layout tool.
  \item \textbf{Exclude fabricated metrological chrome (No fake metrology):} When generating a conceptual morphology placeholder, explicitly instruct the model: \texttt{no instrument data bar, no magnification readout, no fake scale bar}, and stamp ``Model-generated illustration, not a micrograph'' onto the panel immediately upon export.
\end{enumerate}

\chapter{Figure Legend Checklist}
\label{app:legend-checklist}

A figure legend is the evidentiary manual that makes a scientific figure interpretable. No matter how crisp a figure looks on the page, omitting sample sizes, error-bar definitions, scale-bar lengths, or evidence-status disclosures leaves reviewers and readers unable to evaluate the claim. This appendix provides a twelve-point itemized checklist for figure legends together with a side-by-side comparison of an unverified AI legend draft and an author-verified final legend.

\section{Twelve-Point Itemized Legend Checklist}

Before finalizing any manuscript figure, supplementary figure, or poster block, verify its legend item by item against the twelve checks below (parentheses indicate the primary chapters where each rule is demonstrated):

\begin{itemize}
  \item[$\square$] \textbf{1. Opening overview sentence defines the scope (Chapter 9):} The first sentence states the study system or overall phenomenon shown in the composite figure in a concise declarative clause, neither parroting a generic section heading nor overclaiming causal generality beyond the data.
  \item[$\square$] \textbf{2. Every panel letter is accounted for in order (Chapter 9):} Each panel (\textbf{a}, \textbf{b}, \textbf{c}, \textbf{d}, \dots) in a multi-panel composite has a dedicated entry in matching sequence, with letter case and boldface formatting identical between the canvas and the legend text.
  \item[$\square$] \textbf{3. Every visual encoding is defined (Chapters 4 and 5):} All colours, marker shapes, line styles (solid, dashed, dotted), shaded bands, and reference lines appearing in the figure are explained explicitly and match the paper-wide visual specification sheet.
  \item[$\square$] \textbf{4. Statistics and sample sizes come from verified author records (Chapters 5 and 9):} The legend states the exact sample size $n$ for every group, the nature of replication (biological vs.\ technical replicates), the central-tendency measure, the error-bar definition (SD, SEM, or 95\% CI), and the statistical test name with one- or two-sided specification; \textbf{no unverified number drafted by an AI tool is ever retained.}
  \item[$\square$] \textbf{5. Physical quantities, units, and axis transformations are complete (Chapter 5):} Every axis quantity, standard SI unit, logarithmic base, or normalization baseline (for example, ``normalized to initial efficiency at $t = 0$'') is stated explicitly.
  \item[$\square$] \textbf{6. Experimental-image acquisition and processing parameters are disclosed (Chapter 8):} Legends for microscopy or experimental images state the physical length represented by every scale bar together with its calibration basis, staining or fluorescence channel names, imaging modality, and full-frame linear display adjustments, confirming that compared groups share identical intensity windows.
  \item[$\square$] \textbf{7. Evidence status is explicitly declared (Chapters 7, 12, and 13):} Any simulated data, single-diode theoretical curves, model-generated illustrations (explicitly noted as non-micrographs), not-to-scale structural schematics, or working hypotheses are clearly identified in the legend (and inside the panel where required).
  \item[$\square$] \textbf{8. Mechanistic omissions and simplifications are noted (Chapter 7):} Collateral signalling branches, intermediate metabolites, or secondary quality-control steps omitted from a mechanism or workflow diagram to preserve visual clarity are briefly noted at the end of the legend.
  \item[$\square$] \textbf{9. Abbreviations are expanded at first mention (Chapter 9):} Every domain abbreviation appearing inside the figure (such as PCE, FF, NHEJ, HDR, or SHM) is spelled out in full or clearly defined the first time it occurs in the legend.
  \item[$\square$] \textbf{10. External datasets and open-licensed images carry proper attribution (Chapters 1 and 8):} Any panel built from public monitoring networks (such as USGS or NOAA), public bioimage repositories (such as BBBC022), or open-licensed assets includes the required source citation, station or dataset identifier, author credit, and license name.
  \item[$\square$] \textbf{11. Cross-language legends preserve identical factual content (Throughout):} When preparing bilingual materials or adapting figures across languages, both versions contain the exact same experimental conditions, numerical values, scope qualifiers, and evidence-status disclosures, phrased idiomatically in each language.
  \item[$\square$] \textbf{12. \end{itemize}

\chapter{Pre-Submission Delivery Checklist}
\label{app:delivery-checklist}

Between finishing the visual design of a scientific figure and submitting a manuscript---or handing the project folder to a labmate---lies a mandatory stage of quantitative technical verification and archival packaging. Many manuscripts are bounced at journal preflight checks, or stall six months later during peer-review revisions because the authors themselves can no longer locate the raw data tables and plotting parameters. This appendix compiles the single-figure pre-submission technical checklist from Chapter 13, the six-tier delivery package directory specification, the AI-use log template, and the colleague handoff blind-test protocol.

\section{Single-Figure Technical Checklist Before Submission}

Run every figure slated for submission or presentation (main figures, supplementary figures, graphical abstracts, talk slides, and posters) through the seven technical checks below:

\begin{itemize}
  \item[$\square$] \textbf{1. Pixel width, physical print width, and effective-resolution calculation:} For every panel containing raster imagery, record the pixel width and evaluate effective resolution at the target journal column width:
  \[
    \text{Effective resolution (ppi)} = \frac{\text{Pixel width (px)}}{\text{Target physical width (mm)} \div 25.4}
  \]
  Confirm that photographic panels reach at least $300\text{ ppi}$, combination panels (photographs with overlaid line art or text) reach at least $500\text{--}600\text{ ppi}$, and pure line art submitted as a bitmap reaches at least $900\text{--}1200\text{ ppi}$ (or the journal's stated threshold); confirm that no raw experimental image was padded via interpolation or AI super-resolution upscaling.
  \item[$\square$] \textbf{2. 100\% physical printout measurement of font sizes and stroke widths:} Calculate the layout scaling factor ($\text{Final font size} = \text{Canvas font size} \times \text{Final width} \div \text{Canvas width}$) and verify that scaled body labels fall within $7\text{--}9\text{ pt}$ (with subscripts no smaller than the journal's absolute floor of $5\text{ pt}$ or $6\text{ pt}$) and panel letters are $8\text{ pt}$ bold; print the figure at $100\%$ actual size on an A4 sheet, verify column width with a physical ruler, and confirm that the smallest text and thinnest lines ($\ge 0.25\text{--}0.5\text{ pt}$) remain crisp.
  \item[$\square$] \textbf{3. Internal inspection of vector and container files:} Remember that ``PDF, SVG, EPS, and PPTX are container extensions, not guarantees of true vector paths.'' Run a font-inspection utility (such as \texttt{pdffonts}) to confirm that all fonts are embedded without missing glyphs, and run an embedded-image check (such as \texttt{pdfimages -list}) or an $800\%$ zoom inspection to verify that data curves, axes, and text labels are sharp vector geometry rather than rasterized bitmaps.
  \item[$\square$] \textbf{4. Colour space and master-file preservation:} Archive an RGB master file with an embedded standard sRGB profile (the preferred submission colour space for almost all modern journals); convert a separate copy to CMYK only when explicitly required by the publisher, and inspect saturated cyan, green, and blue regions for gamut-clipping dullness.
  \item[$\square$] \textbf{5. Perceptual grayscale lightness separation and colour-vision deficiency simulation:} Convert the figure to perceptual luminance grayscale (CIE $L^*$) and run protanopia, deuteranopia, and tritanopia simulations (such as the Machado et al.\ 2009 physiological matrices) to confirm that every group remains distinguishable via lightness contrast $\Delta L^*$, line style (solid/dashed/dotted), marker shape, or direct end-of-curve labels.
  \item[$\square$] \textbf{6. Automated figure-checker report paired with manual verification:} Upload the exported file to the figure checker module of \scidraw{} at the target journal's exact custom column width (rather than relying solely on the default $85\text{ mm}$ or $180\text{ mm}$ presets) to obtain a technical report on effective resolution, colour space, transparency, grayscale hierarchy, and colour-vision risks; then manually verify every item that automated pixel checks \textbf{cannot evaluate} (actual printed point size, true vector paths inside PDF containers, scientific accuracy of arrows and statistics, and completeness of evidence-status labels).
  \item[$\square$] \textbf{7. Archival copy of official journal guidelines and date checked:} Consult the target journal's current author artwork guidelines and AI-disclosure policy, verify column widths, file formats, font rules, file-size ceilings, and mandatory declaration statements, and save the official URL and access date (YYYY-MM-DD) inside your delivery package.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{File naming rules.}
Name every file inside the delivery package using the fixed pattern \texttt{project\_figure-or-medium\_content-slug\_vNN\_lang} (for example, \texttt{perovskite\_fig2c\_jv\_v05\_en}). Use only lowercase ASCII letters, digits, hyphens, and underscores; never include spaces or ambiguous labels such as ``final'', ``newest'', or \texttt{final\_v2\_new}. Version numbers increment monotonically and are never overwritten; the root \texttt{README} identifies which version number corresponds to the currently submitted manuscript.

\paragraph{Three questions every README must answer.}
The root \texttt{README} need not be longer than one page, but it must answer three concrete questions clearly: (1) \textbf{What is in this package:} the manuscript title, figure number, file locations of the current submission-ready finals, and active version number; (2) \textbf{How to reproduce the figure:} which script in \texttt{02\_Analysis\_and\_Plotting\_Scripts} reads which table in \texttt{01\_Raw\_Materials}, and how panels are assembled into the final composite; and (3) \textbf{Where AI tools were used and how the author verified them:} a pointer to the AI-use log in \texttt{05\_Verification\_Logs}, summarizing which base illustrations or text drafts involved AI assistance and which data curves and labels were produced deterministically and verified by hand.

\begin{BookNote}
Important reminder regarding export and storage boundaries in \scidraw{}: (1) The archive downloaded from the multi-panel figure maker contains exported TIFF, PNG, PDF, and PPTX files together with a processing log, but \textbf{does not bundle} your uploaded raw single-panel master files or a reloadable web project file; layout state lives in your current browser's local storage and cannot be recovered if you switch computers or clear browser cache, so you must archive \texttt{01\_Raw\_Materials} yourself. (2) Step progress and figure briefs inside the figure workflow guide are likewise stored locally in the active browser; export and save a local copy at the end of each stage. (3) In the scientific data visualization module, PDF and EPS exports produced via the declarative chart path wrap high-resolution raster images inside container files; whenever a journal requires true vector paths and editable text for data plots, run your companion deterministic Python script directly on the verified data table to output native vector PDF line art.
\end{BookNote}

\section{Colleague Handoff Blind-Test Protocol}

Once your delivery package is assembled, do not rely solely on self-inspection---because you already hold the project context in your head, you will naturally gloss over missing links in your own documentation. Hand the folder to a labmate who did not participate in making the figure, allow them to read only the root \texttt{README}, and run a 10- to 15-minute blind test:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Ask them, without asking you any clarifying questions, to locate the raw data table, the plotting script, and the exact revision instruction submitted to \scidraw{} for a specified panel (for example, Manuscript Figure 2c);
  \item Ask them to identify which file is the current submission-ready final version and to state which elements of the figure represent simulated data or model-generated illustrations;
  \item Note every wrong subfolder they open and every sentence in the \texttt{README} where they hesitate, revise the \texttt{README} once to eliminate those ambiguities, and confirm that an uninvolved colleague can complete the trace within 10 minutes.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Technical Specifications of the Simulated Datasets}

To teach foundational data visualization, multi-panel assembly, cross-medium adaptation, and pre-submission technical auditing, the book relies on two original simulated datasets generated entirely by deterministic code. Their purpose is to let every reader reproduce every number, curve, and verification check in the book with $100\%$ precision without mistaking a pedagogical exercise for an empirical scientific discovery.

\paragraph{1. Eight-subject paired-measurement simulated dataset (Method A).}
This dataset contains paired measurements for eight fictional study units (\texttt{SIM01} through \texttt{SIM08}) evaluated at two conditions (\texttt{before} and \texttt{after}), expressed in arbitrary units (a.u.) across 8 rows and 16 integer observations with zero missing values. The eight pairs are: \texttt{SIM01} ($12 \rightarrow 16$), \texttt{SIM02} ($15 \rightarrow 18$), \texttt{SIM03} ($9 \rightarrow 13$), \texttt{SIM04} ($18 \rightarrow 17$), \texttt{SIM05} ($11 \rightarrow 15$), \texttt{SIM06} ($14 \rightarrow 19$), \texttt{SIM07} ($16 \rightarrow 20$), and \texttt{SIM08} ($10 \rightarrow 14$). Its summary statistics are constructed deliberately: the pre-treatment mean is $13.125\text{ a.u.}$ and the post-treatment mean is $16.500\text{ a.u.}$; seven of the eight units increase after treatment (by $3\text{ to }5\text{ a.u.}$), whereas \texttt{SIM04} moves in the opposite direction, dropping from $18\text{ a.u.}$ to $17\text{ a.u.}$. This ``seven up, one down'' structure serves throughout the book as a diagnostic test of whether a chart preserves paired trajectories and individual counter-trends---a standalone mean bar chart conceals \texttt{SIM04}'s reversal completely. The companion plotting script reads the table directly without generating, smoothing, or altering any observation.

\paragraph{2. Perovskite solar cell performance and stability simulated dataset (Case PSC).}
Supporting Chapter 9 (multi-panel assembly and legends), Chapter 12 (talk slides and A0 poster adaptation), and Chapter 13 (resolution, vector containers, colour-vision simulation, and handoff archiving), this dataset comprises two deterministic tables comparing a \texttt{control} group and an interfacially passivated \texttt{modified} group for a planar n-i-p perovskite solar cell:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Single-diode current density--voltage ($J$--$V$) characteristics:} Evaluated under standard AM1.5G illumination ($P_{\mathrm{in}} = 100\text{ mW cm}^{-2}$) at a cell temperature of $25\ ^\circ\text{C}$ (thermal voltage $V_{\mathrm{T}} = k_{\mathrm{B}}T/q = 0.025693\text{ V}$), the output current density $J$ at each bias voltage $V$ (spanning $0\text{ to }1.20\text{ V}$ in $0.5\text{ mV}$ steps) is obtained from the implicit single-diode equation:

    J = J_{\mathrm{L}} - J_0 \left[\exp\!\left(\frac{V + J R_{\mathrm{s}}}{n V_{\mathrm{T}}}\right) - 1\right] - \frac{V + J R_{\mathrm{s}}}{R_{\mathrm{sh}}}

\section{Scaled Font Sizes and Viewing-Angle Equivalent Font Sizes}

\paragraph{1. Canvas-scaling font-size formula for manuscript figures.}
When you draw a figure on a canvas of width $W_{\text{canvas}}$ using a nominal font size $S_{\text{canvas}}$ and subsequently scale the exported image proportionally to fit a journal column width $W_{\text{final}}$, the printed font size seen by the reader is:
\[
  S_{\text{final}} = S_{\text{canvas}} \times \frac{W_{\text{final}}}{W_{\text{canvas}}}
\]
\textbf{Worked trap example:} Drawing a plot in a default $7.3\text{ in}$ ($185.4\text{ mm}$) software window with $10\text{ pt}$ axis labels and scaling the image down to an $85\text{ mm}$ single column reduces the printed type to $10 \times 85 / 185.4 \approx 4.6\text{ pt}$---below the $5\text{--}7\text{ pt}$ minimum enforced by nearly all journals. The safest workflow is to \textbf{set your plotting canvas width directly to the target journal column width} (such as $85\text{ mm}$ or $180\text{ mm}$) and specify $7\text{ pt}$ axis labels and $8\text{ pt}$ bold panel letters at $1{:}1$ scale.

\paragraph{2. Cross-medium viewing-angle equivalent font sizes and line widths.}
Whether a viewer can read a label depends on the visual angle $\theta \approx h/d$ subtended by the character height $h$ at viewing distance $d$. Taking $9\text{ pt}$ body text read at $d_0 = 40\text{ cm}$ ($0.4\text{ m}$, visual angle $\approx 0.45^\circ$) as our comfortable reading baseline, the equivalent point size required at distance $d$ scales linearly as $S(d) = 9\text{ pt} \times (d / 0.4\text{ m})$. For a standard 16:9 widescreen slide of vertical height $7.5\text{ in}$ ($540\text{ pt}$), when the ratio of back-row viewing distance $D$ to projected screen height $H$ is $D/H$, maintaining a $0.45^\circ$ visual angle requires a minimum slide font size of $S_{\text{slide}} \approx 0.00785 \times (D/H) \times 540\text{ pt}$.
